Cat care guide

Signs your cat is getting older — and what to watch for

Cats are good at hiding change, and the signs your cat is getting older are rarely dramatic. They show up in how often a cat jumps, how much water disappears, and whether the coat still looks cared for.

7 min read General care information, not veterinary advice

The first signs your cat is getting older are usually easy to miss. Cats are generally considered senior from around age 11, with mature adulthood starting near age 7 and an advanced-senior label from 15. But the body does not wait for a label, so the changes below are worth watching from middle age onward — early changes are easier to discuss with a vet than late ones.

Weight and muscle are early signs your cat is getting older

Weigh your cat monthly if you can, and run a hands-on check: you should feel the ribs without pressing hard, see a waist from above, and feel steady muscle along the spine and hindquarters.

Weight loss in an older cat is not automatically “just aging.” Dental disease, digestive issues, kidney disease, thyroid disease, and diabetes can all play a role. A gradual change in muscle along the spine can also happen while the scale stays stable.

Water and litter box signs as cats get older

  • Notice whether water bowls are emptied faster or the cat asks for running water more often.
  • Larger or more frequent urine clumps are worth a vet conversation.
  • Watch for straining, accidents outside the box, or a cat who avoids the box because the sides are too high.
  • Keep water in several places and consider a low-sided box as mobility changes.

Mobility changes when a cat is getting older

  • Hesitation before jumping onto a favorite chair or windowsill
  • Shorter jumps, or climbing in stages rather than in one motion
  • Stiffness after sleeping, or a quieter interest in play
  • Using the litter box less reliably because getting in is harder

Pain and arthritis are common in older cats, but they are not always obvious. A vet can assess gait, joints, and comfort. Ramps, low boxes, and soft bedding can make the home easier in the meantime.

Home adjustments work best before they are strictly needed. A cat who learns a ramp early keeps using it; a cat already in pain may refuse anything new. Introduce steps and low-sided boxes while jumping is still possible, and keep the locations consistent so an older cat does not have to renegotiate the house.

Grooming and coat changes in an older cat

A cat who stops grooming thoroughly may develop mats, dandruff, or a greasy coat. Gentle brushing helps, and it is also a good way to notice weight loss, new lumps, or sore spots. Dental pain and arthritis can both reduce grooming, so a scruffy coat is sometimes an early clue rather than a cosmetic issue. Grooming changes like these are often among the most visible signs your cat is getting older, so they are worth noting even when they seem minor.

Sleep and routine in older cats

More sleep is normal. Changes in the pattern are more informative: night yowling, pacing, confusion, staring at walls, or a cat who seems unsettled after dark. Keep meals, play, and bedtime predictable, and leave a night light on if the cat seems disoriented.

Appetite and dental health in older cats

  • Eating less, or preferring soft food when crunchy food was fine before
  • Dropping food, chewing on one side, or pawing at the mouth
  • Bad breath, drooling, or visible tartar

Dental disease is common in older cats and can affect eating and comfort. It is worth asking about at routine checkups rather than waiting for a cat to stop eating.

When the signs of getting older usually appear

Most cats show their first real changes between seven and eleven, in the mature adult stage, and the pace picks up after eleven. Early on, the shifts are easy to wave away: a little less jumping, a slightly rougher coat, longer naps. By the senior stage, the same areas — weight, mobility, hydration, grooming, sleep — move from background noise to a useful checklist, and by fifteen most cats have at least one change worth tracking with a vet.

The order is not fixed. One cat loses muscle before anything else; another drinks more water months before the scale moves. That is why a broad monthly look beats watching for any single symptom: the pattern across several small changes is usually the real story of a cat getting older.

Common mistakes with an older cat

  • Writing weight loss off as normal aging. In older cats, weight loss is a reason to investigate, not a milestone to accept.
  • Waiting for obvious pain. Cats rarely cry or limp dramatically; hiding, sleeping in new spots, and skipping jumps are their version of saying something hurts.
  • Assuming a finicky appetite is pickiness. Dental disease and nausea are common, treatable reasons an older cat goes off food.
  • Keeping one high-sided litter box in the basement. If getting to the box is hard, accidents are a housing problem, not a behavior problem.
  • Skipping checkups because the cat seems fine. Kidney and thyroid changes are often invisible at home until they are advanced.

None of these make anyone a bad owner — cats are genuinely hard to read. The fix is structural: weigh monthly, watch the litter box, and treat any sustained change as information rather than personality.

A monthly tracking routine for an older cat

  • Weigh your cat, or do a hands-on rib and spine check, and write the result down
  • Review the litter box: clump size, frequency, and any accidents
  • Note water intake roughly — is the bowl emptying faster than last month
  • Brush once and check for mats, lumps, sore spots, and ear or mouth changes
  • Watch one jump or stair climb and compare it with a few months ago
  • Jot down anything new — sleep pattern, voice, appetite — for the next vet visit

Make daily life easier for an older cat

A few small changes to the layout of the home remove most of the daily friction an older cat faces. Start with the essentials: litter access, safe routes to favorite spots, warmth, and food and water that do not require climbing stairs.

  • Put a litter box on every floor the cat uses, with low sides.
  • Add a ramp or step to favorite high spots, and keep landing areas clear.
  • Provide warm, soft sleeping places away from drafts.
  • Keep food and water within easy reach, especially if stairs are difficult.
  • Brush gently and check paws, ears, and mouth as part of the routine.

When an older cat needs a vet visit

  • Weight loss, muscle loss, or a change in appetite that lasts more than a week or two
  • A sudden increase in drinking or urination
  • Vomiting, diarrhea, or constipation that persists
  • New breathing difficulty, coughing, or lethargy
  • Disorientation, night yowling, or a change in social behavior
  • Any new lump, wound, or sign of pain

Scope note: The signs your cat is getting older described here are common changes, not diagnoses. Cats are individuals, and many conditions share the same early symptoms. A veterinarian is the right person to interpret a change.

Senior cat care FAQ

What are the first signs of aging in cats?

The earliest signs are usually subtle: less jumping, more sleeping, a slightly unkempt coat, and small changes in weight or muscle. Because cats hide discomfort well, monthly weighing and a simple body-condition check are often more informative than waiting for obvious symptoms.

Why is my senior cat losing weight?

Weight loss in an older cat has many possible causes, including dental disease, digestive problems, kidney disease, thyroid disease, diabetes, and stress. It should not be assumed to be a normal part of aging. A veterinarian can examine the cat and recommend appropriate testing.

How often should a senior cat see the vet?

From mature adulthood onward, many veterinarians recommend a checkup every six months. Senior cats often benefit from regular weight, dental, kidney, thyroid, and blood-pressure monitoring. Your vet can set a schedule based on the individual cat.
